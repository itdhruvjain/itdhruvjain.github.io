Evidence — the work nobody asked for

A report that took ten minutes now takes ten seconds.

PIQET is a ten-year-old packaging life-cycle-assessment product. Its calculations lived in large, deeply nested SQL stored procedures, and the pipeline had run untouched for three years because the numbers are what the customer acts on — the downside of breaking them was real.

What I changed

Moved the calculation layer out of the stored procedures and into the API behind a single source of truth, then added Redis caching for the repeated inputs. Nobody asked for it — every lead before me had built around those procedures rather than into them.

How I knew it was safe

The whole risk here was correctness, so I proved it before cutover: replayed historical reports through both paths and diffed every output field to the decimal. The rewrite shipped only once the two agreed on all of them.

What happened after

The rewrite has been in production since cutover, and the report numbers are still the ones the client acts on. I lead a team of three on the product and own the client relationship directly.

Evidence — can you pick up our stack?

Legacy codebase to modern stack, shipped in two months.

Health4Community is a multi-tenant SaaS platform for healthcare and clinic organisations — three systems under one roof, running on a legacy ASP.NET MVC codebase. Leading five engineers, I re-architected it onto .NET 10 (Clean Architecture, DDD) and Angular 20 and shipped the new version in two months.

The scope, so you can judge it

Three systems inside one platform: billing, document management and SOP control, and HR with rostering — the last including automatic roster generation.

Serving three client organisations and around 50 users at cutover.

What I own on it

The multi-tenant foundations — fine-grained permissions, tenant isolation, Azure AD B2C identity, per-tenant branding — and the whole HR and workforce side: attendance, timesheets with multi-level approvals, leave, rostering, configurable overtime and award loading.

The part that was actually hard

Auto-building rosters. A generated roster has to satisfy availability, shift overlaps, approval chains, configurable overtime and award-rule variations at the same time — and still let a manager override any cell by hand without the rest falling apart. I modelled those constraints before anyone hit them, which is why later requirements fitted the model instead of forcing a rewrite.
